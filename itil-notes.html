ITIL & ITSM

ITIL is a framework of practices and guidelines used to improve the value of the services an organization offers.

ITSM (IT Service Management) is the set of activities an organization performs to design, build, deliver, operate, and control the IT services it offers to customers.

ITIL vs. ITSM — ITIL is one framework that teaches best practices for implementing ITSM; ITSM is the overall organizational implementation that delivers IT services. In short: ITIL describes how to do it, ITSM describes what to do.

Key terminology

Process
A step-by-step procedure.
Service
A means of delivering value to customers by achieving the results they want.
Function
A set of people or a set of groups.
SLA — Service Level Agreement
A legally authenticated agreement between client and service provider, defining service level targets and each party's responsibilities, to avoid ambiguity.
OLA — Operational Level Agreement
An agreement between the IT service provider and another part of the same organization.
UC — Underpinning Contract
An agreement between the IT service provider and a third party.

1. Service Strategy

Requirement gathering and analysis happen here — analyzing the business, demand, supply, and portfolio, and helping the organization decide which services to offer and which markets to target.

  • Financial management
  • Demand management
  • Service portfolio management
  • Business relationship management
  • Strategy management

This is where the planning of the service (product) happens, across eight areas of management.

  • Service catalogue management
  • Service level management
  • Capacity management
  • Availability management
  • IT service continuity management
  • Information security management
  • Supplier management
  • Design coordination

3. Service Transition

Developing, implementing, testing, and releasing happens here, across six services/management areas.

  • Knowledge management
  • Service assets and configuration management
  • Change management
  • Release and deploy management
  • Service validation and testing management
  • Transition planning and support management

Change management

Change is the addition, modification, or removal of anything that could affect IT services. Change management controls the lifecycle of all changes, enabling beneficial changes with minimum disruption to IT services. Every change needs a change ticket — called an RFC (Request For Change) or CR (Change Request).

CAB — Change Advisory Board
The team that approves or rejects a change before it releases to production. Contains technical approvers and business/financial/functional approvers.
ECAB — Emergency CAB
Used for emergency changes.
PIR — Post Implementation Review
Reviews the change after implementation, by both technical and business teams.

Release & deploy management

Release management handles the release process into production, including deployment — done by a separate build/release team, a developer, or a production support member. Whoever raises the change is responsible for assigning the release task to the right team. Release instructions live in the release tracker, and the person deploying must follow them. If a release causes issues, it can be rolled back.

Note — every change must contain at least one task.

4. Service Operation

Five processes:

  • Incident management
  • Problem management
  • Event management
  • Request fulfilment management
  • Access management

Incident management

An incident is any unexpected deviation from the normal running of a service. Incident management restores service as soon as possible, without compromising the SLA.

Priority is set by impact and urgency:

Incident priority matrix
PriorityImpactUrgencyRespond timeResolution time
P1 — CriticalWidespread / significantCritical≤ 15 min≤ 1 hour
P2 — HighSignificantMedium / high≤ 1 hour≤ 4 hours
P3 — MediumModerate / limitedMedium≤ 4 hours≤ 1 day
P4 — LowLocalized / limitedLow≤ 8 hours≤ 2 days

Incident lifecycle:

Open→ In Progress→ Pending→ Resolved→ Closed

The ticket opens to a group or person (Open), moves to In Progress once someone acknowledges and works it (this is what satisfies the respond SLA), and can move to Pending if it needs help from a third party — this stops the SLA clock, which restarts when it goes back to In Progress. It's then marked Resolved, and automatically moves to Closed after a set span of time.

Note — a resolved incident can be re-opened; a closed incident cannot.

Problem management

A problem is any issue whose root cause is unknown — it may have occurred once or repeatedly. The main goal of problem management is finding the root cause and delivering a permanent fix.

A workaround is the temporary process used to resolve the issue in the meantime, followed until the root cause is found. Once the root cause is known, the problem is called a PKE (Problem Known Error), and changes are implemented to fix it — through the standard change management process.

A separate problem resolution document is maintained, covering reference incidents, business impact, changes advised, and estimated fix time.

Event management

An event is any significant occurrence within an IT environment. Event types are informational rather than mandatory to act on:

  • Alert
  • Warning
  • Exception
  • Notification

Request fulfilment & access management

A request is any normal ask from a user or group — one that won't impact production, such as an update, modification, or a request for data or information. This is handled under request fulfilment management.

Access management covers access-related activity: granting permissions, creating new users, and similar tasks.

5. Continual Service Improvement (CSI)

CSI aims to improve the efficiency and economy of every process involved in delivering services — identifying opportunities to improve services, processes, and tools. Its core approach is figuring out how to keep momentum going, and what measurable targets and improvements are needed for services and processes.
